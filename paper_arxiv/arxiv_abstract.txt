LLM judges are used to rank models, to provide reward signals for training, and to monitor other models. Their outputs are usually anonymised, on the assumption that a judge cannot recognise its own text. This assumption is typically tested by checking whether a judge names itself as the author of its own text more often than chance. We show that this test is unreliable: a high self-naming rate can also arise when a text is easy for any model to attribute, or when a judge names itself on most texts it sees. We instead compare each judge's self-naming rate with two controls available from the same experiment: its false-alarm rate on texts it did not write, and a peer baseline, the rate at which the other judges name it as the author of its texts. We evaluate nine judges (five frontier and four open-weight models) on 1,270 texts in four genres, collecting more than 19,000 authorship judgments. The standard test detects self-recognition in 15 cases, of which 4 also pass our test. The largest effects are in the lineup format, where Claude and GPT exceed their peer baselines by 33 and 28 percentage points on essays written in their chat apps. Yet neither passes our test on a second set of 600 texts from the same models, and the results change with the question format. When asked directly whether they wrote a text, GPT, Grok and DeepSeek answered no for all 190 essays, including their own. A style classifier correctly attributes 86% of the essays, and each open-weight judge assigns the highest relative likelihood to its own text on every prompt, but the judges' answers do not track either signal. These results motivate a simple audit for anonymised evaluation that uses only the judgments a lineup experiment already collects.
