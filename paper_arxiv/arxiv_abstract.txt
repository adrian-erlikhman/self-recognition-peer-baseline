Anonymised LLM evaluation assumes that judges cannot identify their own outputs. Self-recognition is usually assessed by testing whether a judge attributes its own text to itself more often than chance. However, above-chance self-attribution may reflect a tendency to claim authorship regardless of the source, or writing that other models can identify just as readily. We distinguish these possibilities using two controls: a false-alarm rate measuring how often a judge claims other models' texts, and a peer baseline measuring how often other judges identify its writing, which reflects other-recognition. We evaluate nine LLM judges (five frontier and four open-weight) on 1,270 texts across four genres, collecting over 19,000 authorship judgments. The self-naming test flags 15 of 37 cases, but 7 of them fail to distinguish the judge's own texts from others'. Of the remaining 8, all involving frontier models (Claude asked yes/no only with correction within its panel), only 3 significantly outperform the pooled peer baseline, and none significantly outperforms its strongest individual peer. In the lineup, Claude names itself on 33 of its 38 chat-app essays, and GPT names Claude on 32 of them. The texts carry authorship information that the judges' answers do not reflect: a style classifier attributes 86% of the chat-app essays, and each open-weight judge's own likelihood ranks its text first on 58.3% to 86.7% of prompts, yet those judges are near chance in the lineup. These findings show that although some LLM judges can distinguish their own writing, above-chance self-attribution alone does not establish a self-specific recognition advantage. Evaluations of LLM self-recognition must account for both false attribution and how readily other models recognise the same texts.
