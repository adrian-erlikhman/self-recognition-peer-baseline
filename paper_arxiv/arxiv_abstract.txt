Anonymised LLM evaluation assumes that a judge cannot recognise its own text. The usual test asks whether a judge names itself as the author of its own texts more often than chance. A judge can pass this test without recognising anything: it may name itself on most texts it sees, or its texts may be easy for any model to attribute. We test for both with data the same lineup experiment already collects: the judge's false-alarm rate on other models' texts, a correction from recent work, and a new peer baseline, the rate at which the other judges name it as the author of its texts. Nine judges (five frontier, four open-weight) attribute 1,270 texts in four genres, giving more than 19,000 judgments. The self-naming test flags 15 of 37 cases (one judge, one set of texts, one question format). The false-alarm control removes 7, and among frontier judges the peer baseline removes 4 of the 5 that fail. 3 cases pass both controls, plus one yes/no case that has no peer baseline. In the lineup, Claude names itself on 87% of its own chat-app essays and 9% of others', and Claude and GPT exceed their peer baselines by 33 and 28 points. On API texts for the same prompts, GPT's discrimination falls; Claude still names itself far more often on its own texts than on others', but its peers name it more often too, so its advantage over them is unclear. Asked in a new conversation whether they wrote a text, GPT, Grok and DeepSeek answered no for all 190 essays, including their own. The texts carry authorship information that the judges do not use: a style classifier attributes 86% of the essays, and each open-weight judge's own likelihood ranks its text first on 58.3% to 86.7% of prompts even without the prompt, yet those judges are at chance in the lineup. These results motivate a simple audit for anonymised evaluation that uses only the judgments a lineup experiment already collects.
