LLM judges rank models on leaderboards, supply the rewards that train them, and monitor other models for unsafe behaviour. The standard protection against a judge favouring its own outputs is to hide who wrote each answer, and whether that protection holds is usually checked with one number: how often a judge names itself as the author of its own text. We show that this check is badly miscalibrated. A high self-naming rate can reflect genuine recognition, but also text that any model can attribute, or a judge that says "me" to almost everything. We score each judge against two references the same experiment already provides: how often it names itself on text it did not write, and how often the other judges name it on its text. Across nine judges, 1,270 texts in four genres and more than 19,000 judgments, the usual check reports self-recognition in 15 cases, and 11 of them do not survive. Genuine self-recognition does exist (Claude and GPT beat their peers by 33 and 28 points on chat-app essays), but it disappears on a second set of 600 texts, and the question format alone can create or erase it: asked directly, three frontier judges deny writing any of 190 texts, their own included. The authorship signal is plainly present, in the style of the text and in each open-weight judge's own likelihoods, yet the judges' answers do not follow it. Anonymisation therefore has to be tested per judge, on the texts a pipeline actually judges, against a peer baseline. We release all data and an audit that needs no extra data.
