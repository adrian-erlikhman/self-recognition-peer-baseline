\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
\label{sec:recs}
\label{sec:limits}

The self-naming test conflates two questions, and the two controls separate
them. Frontier judges can recognise their own text, but none measurably better
than its strongest peer: much of what looks like self-recognition is
other-recognition. The first question is whether a judge recognises its own
text at all. Of the \CVflagged{} cases the self-naming test flags, the
false-alarm control removes \CVfailFA{}, almost all open-weight cases in which
the judge names itself regardless of the author; in the remaining \CVdisc{},
all frontier, the judge distinguishes its own texts from others'. The second
question is whether that recognition is the judge's own, and here the evidence
is weak. Only \CVbothAttr{} cases, all on 38 chat-app essays per judge, beat
the pooled peers (\CXLikeForLike{} under the like-for-like comparison, one of
them on API texts). None is significantly better than its strongest single
peer, though that test can detect only large advantages, and on the 600 API
texts no case passes both controls with the pooled baseline. Claude
distinguishes its own texts in every setting we tested (in the yes/no format
only with correction within the panel), but GPT names Claude on 32 of Claude's
38 chat-app essays, against Claude's own 33. The question format determines
whether a judge names itself at all, and the judges' explicit attributions
reflect little of the authorship information in stylometric features and in
their own likelihoods.

\paragraph{Implications}
Other-recognition matters in its own right. Recognising oneself is a special
case of attributing text to models, and a developer could make it reliable by
giving its judge the detector for a watermark it controls; recognising another
developer's model cannot be solved that way. In the chat-app lineup,
anonymisation hides Claude's authorship from neither Claude nor GPT, so a
pipeline in which one model judges another's outputs can face the same
identification problem as one in which a model judges its own. Measured
self-recognition depends jointly on the judge, the texts it evaluates and the
question format, so a result on one benchmark may not hold for the same model
in general, and a model update may change it. \citet{bai2025selfrecognition}
observe that much work on self-preference presupposes self-recognition, and
\citet{panickssery2024selfpreference} propose recognition as one of its causes.
We find frontier judges that recognise their own text, but, consistent with
\citet{davidson2024anonymized} and \citet{bai2025selfrecognition}, little
evidence that this recognition is self-specific. Bai et al.\ conclude that
recognition is unlikely to be the main reason for self-preference. Our data
cannot test that conclusion: no frontier judge rated or ranked the texts, and
the open-weight ratings are compressed near the top of the scale. A judge
that fails an attribution test may still favour its own text, since
self-preference can arise from familiarity without recognition
\citep{wataoka2024perplexity}. For a judge that distinguishes its own text,
such as Claude in every setting we tested, removing the author's name does not
fully hide authorship, whether or not its peers can also identify the texts.
Whether the judge then favours those texts is a separate question that we do
not measure. The self-naming test does not distinguish such judges from those
that name themselves on most texts, and it does not tell an evaluator whether
another model in the pipeline could identify the same texts.

\paragraph{Recommendations}
(1)~Report the false-alarm rate and the peer baseline alongside the self-naming
rate, and require evidence of discrimination before interpreting a high
self-naming rate as recognition, and of a self-advantage before calling it
self-specific. Report the two controls separately: discrimination bears on
whether anonymisation protects a judge, and self-advantage on whether the
judge identifies its texts better than other judges can. Report the peer
control as a pooled baseline, like-for-like, and against the strongest single
peer, since a pooled baseline can hide one peer that matches the judge. (2)~Use the lineup format rather than the one-text
format, in which attributions concentrate on one or two models, and rotate the
order of the answers: on the chat-app essays, lineup accuracy is
\CRAccListedChat{} when the answers follow the order in which the names are
listed and \CRAccOtherChat{} otherwise. Before testing any judge, check that
its answers vary; in \CXFixedCells{} of our \CVcells{} cases the judge gives
the same answer on at least 97\% of texts. Use a yes/no question only for
judges whose answers vary across texts. (3)~Evaluate each
judge on the kind of text it will judge, and repeat the evaluation when the judge
or the texts change. (4)~Do not treat stated reasons alone as evidence of what drives
attribution; in our data they are the same for correct and incorrect
self-attributions. Our released code implements this procedure.

\paragraph{Future work}
Because the texts contain authorship information that current judges do not
use, future judges may show stronger self-recognition if they are trained to
use stylistic or likelihood features, given labelled examples, which raised
one judge's macro F1 on model-family attribution from 0.71 to 0.996
\citep{dietrich2026splits}, or given access to their own probabilities.
Self-recognition should therefore be measured again for new model versions.
The two controls apply directly to pairwise
preference judgments and to safety monitoring, and can be extended to longer and
non-English texts. Analysis of model representations
\citep{ackerman2025inspection} may explain why some judges discriminate on some
genres but not on others.

\paragraph{Limitations}
The chat-app essays include only 38 texts per judge, so their confidence
intervals are wide; the API texts include 120 per judge. The comparison between
the two sets rests on the \CSprompts{} prompts they share and cannot settle
whether Claude's self-advantage carries over to API texts. The open-weight
judges have 4 to 8 billion parameters, run with 4-bit weights, are much weaker
attributors than frontier models, and their lineup answers largely follow the
position of a response. No frontier provider returned token probabilities, so
the frontier yes/no results are based on discrete answers, and the likelihood,
quality and answer-first analyses cover only the open-weight judges; we
collected no preference or quality judgments from the frontier judges. All
texts are in English, and every prompt specifies the content of the answer.
Each format was run with one instruction wording and one order of candidate
names, and the lineups used only five answer orders, so the levels of lineup
accuracy reported here include an effect of name order; the one-text format, in
which GPT and Claude are the first two listed names and the most frequent
answers, was not run with other name orders. The lineup and one-text
instructions do not tell a judge that it is one of the listed models, and the
yes/no question is asked without any record of the judge's earlier outputs, so
a refusal to claim a text in that format may reflect the judge's stance on
memory more than its reading of the text. The chat-app essays were collected in
consumer applications whose system prompts we cannot observe, and three of the
applications labelled the writing model as a product variant (``Limited GPT-5.5
Instant'', ``Fast Grok 4.3'', ``Deepseek Instant'') that we cannot verify to be
identical to the API model used as judge. The rule-based coding of the judges'
reasons was validated against two LLM annotators, both instances of Claude,
which is also one of the judges, and not against human annotators. Finally, all
results refer to specific model versions in 2026; providers update models under
the same names, and the DeepSeek endpoint could not be pinned to a snapshot.

\section*{Ethics Statement}
The study involves no human participants or personal data; all texts were
written by language models in response to our prompts. Frontier texts were
collected through the providers' applications and APIs under their terms of use,
and the open-weight models were used under their licences. We name the judges
that distinguish their own texts from others' so that practitioners can take
this into account.
The results apply to specific model versions and may not hold for later
versions of the same models.
