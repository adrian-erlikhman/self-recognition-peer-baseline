\section{What this means for LLM-judge pipelines}
\label{sec:recs}

Our results cut both ways, and both matter. The evidence usually offered for
self-recognition, a self-naming rate above chance, was wrong in \SVPFalse{} of
\SVPStd{} cases in our data. But where we did
find genuine self-recognition, it was large (a 33-point advantage for Claude)
and invisible to anyone who looked at only one format or one set of texts.
Anonymisation is therefore neither safe by default nor broken by default; it
has to be measured. We recommend:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Report three numbers, not one.} A self-naming rate means
little without the false-alarm rate and the peer baseline, and all three come
from the same experiment. Test discrimination first, since that is what
breaks anonymisation; claim self-recognition only if the judge also beats its
peers.
\item \textbf{Do not measure recognition one text at a time.} In that format
judges pile their guesses onto one or two models, three of our five frontier
judges never named themselves, and the peer baseline is inflated for whichever
model everyone guesses. Use a lineup. A yes/no question works only if the
judge's answers vary: three of our frontier judges said no to everything.
\item \textbf{Test each judge on the texts it will judge.} Self-advantage
ranged from strongly positive to significantly negative within one panel, and
disappeared for the same judges on a different set of texts. A result on one
corpus does not transfer.
\item \textbf{Do not read the mechanism off the judges' reasons.} The reasons
describe the text accurately but are the same for right and wrong
self-attributions.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent In practice: have every judge attribute a sample of the pipeline's
own texts in lineups, compute the three rates per judge, and test
discrimination and self-advantage with permutations within prompts. Any
judge that discriminates can see through anonymisation on those texts. Our
released code implements these steps.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Sample size.} The chat-app study has 38 texts per judge, so its
intervals are wide (Table~\ref{tab:frontier}). The larger API study has 120
per judge.

\paragraph{Judges.} The open-weight judges are small (4--8 billion
parameters, run in 4-bit) and much weaker than frontier models. No frontier
provider returned token probabilities, so the frontier yes/no results are
hard answers, and the likelihood, quality and answer-first analyses were run
on the open-weight judges only.

\paragraph{Texts.} All texts are in English, and every prompt fixes the
content of the answer. Dialogue, long documents and other languages are
untested. The chat-app essays were collected in consumer apps whose hidden
system prompts and settings we cannot see.

\paragraph{Reasons.} The coding of the judges' reasons was checked against
two LLM annotators, both instances of Claude, which is also one of the judges.
No human labelled them.

\paragraph{Model versions.} Every result describes particular model versions
at particular dates in 2026. Providers update models behind stable names, and
the DeepSeek endpoint could not be pinned. The audit should be repeated
whenever a judge changes.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Self-recognition in LLM judges is usually measured with a number that cannot
tell recognition from bias. A judge that names itself as the author of its
own text has not thereby shown that it recognises that text. Scored against
its own false alarms and against its peers, most apparent self-recognition
disappears: of the \SVPStd{} cases the usual check flags, only \SVPProper{}
hold up. Those that do are real and large, Claude and GPT beating their peers
by 33 and 28 points on chat-app essays, yet they vanish on a second set of
texts from the same models, and the question format alone can create or erase
them. Meanwhile the information a judge would need to recognise itself is
already in the text and in its own probabilities, unused. Self-recognition is
therefore not a fixed property of a model that can be checked once: it depends
on the judge, the texts and the question. Any pipeline that relies on
anonymisation should test each judge, on the texts it actually judges,
against a peer baseline, and repeat the test whenever the judge or the texts
change. The audit costs nothing beyond the judgments a lineup already
produces.

\section*{Ethics statement}
The study involves no human subjects or personal data: every text was written
by a language model in response to our prompts. The frontier texts were
collected through the providers' apps and APIs under their terms of use, and
the open-weight models were run under their licences. We name the judges that
recognise their own text so that practitioners can account for them; the
results describe specific model versions and should not be read as permanent
properties of a vendor's models.
