\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
\label{sec:recs}
\label{sec:limits}

We evaluated self-recognition in nine LLM judges on three sets of texts and in
three question formats. The standard test, a self-naming rate above chance,
detects self-recognition in \SVPStd{} cases, and \SVPProper{} of these also
show a self-advantage over the peer baseline and discrimination against the
false-alarm rate. The largest effects are in the lineup, where Claude and GPT
exceed their peer baselines by 33 and 28 points on the chat-app essays. The
same judges do not pass our test on 600 API texts, and the question format
determines whether a judge names itself at all. Both stylometric features and
the open-weight judges' likelihoods are strongly informative about authorship,
but the judges' explicit attributions align poorly with these signals.

\paragraph{Implications}
Our results suggest that measured self-recognition depends jointly on the
judge, the texts it evaluates and the question format, so a result on one
benchmark may not hold for the same model in general. A judge that shows no
self-recognition on one benchmark may show it on another, and a model update
may change the result. Prior work on self-preference often assumes that judges
recognise their own outputs. We find little evidence for this assumption in
most of the conditions we tested, consistent with
\citet{davidson2024anonymized} and \citet{bai2025selfrecognition}.
Self-recognition should therefore not be assumed to explain self-preference
without a separate attribution test. For the judges that do recognise their
own texts, anonymisation does not prevent self-preference, and the standard test
does not distinguish these judges from those that only appear to recognise
themselves.

\paragraph{Recommendations}
(1)~Report the false-alarm rate and the peer baseline alongside the self-naming
rate, and require evidence of both discrimination and a self-advantage before
interpreting a high self-naming rate as self-recognition. (2)~Use the lineup format rather than the one-text
format, in which attributions concentrate on one or two models. Use a yes/no
question only for judges whose answers vary across texts. (3)~Evaluate each
judge on the kind of text it will judge, and repeat the evaluation when the judge
or the texts change. (4)~Do not treat stated reasons alone as evidence of what drives
attribution; in our data they are the same for correct and incorrect
self-attributions.
Our released code implements this procedure.

\paragraph{Future work}
Because the texts contain authorship information that current judges do not
use, future judges may show stronger self-recognition if they are trained to
use stylistic or likelihood features, given few-shot examples \citep{dietrich2026splits}, or
given access to their own probabilities. Self-recognition should therefore be
measured again for new model versions. The test applies directly to pairwise
preference judgments and to safety monitoring, and can be extended to longer and
non-English texts. Analysis of model representations
\citep{ackerman2025inspection} may explain why some judges discriminate on some
genres but not on others.

\paragraph{Limitations}
The chat-app essays include only 38 texts per judge, so their confidence
intervals are wide; the API texts include 120 per judge. The open-weight judges
have 4 to 8 billion parameters, run with 4-bit weights, and are much weaker
attributors than frontier models. No frontier provider returned token
probabilities, so the frontier yes/no results are based on discrete answers, and
the likelihood, quality and answer-first analyses cover only the open-weight
judges. All texts are in English, and every prompt specifies the content of the
answer. The chat-app essays were collected in consumer applications whose system
prompts we cannot observe. The rule-based coding of the judges' reasons was
validated against two LLM annotators, both instances of Claude, which is also one
of the judges, and not against human annotators. Finally, all results refer to
specific model versions in 2026; providers update models under the same names,
and the DeepSeek endpoint could not be pinned to a snapshot.

\section*{Ethics Statement}
The study involves no human participants or personal data; all texts were
written by language models in response to our prompts. Frontier texts were
collected through the providers' applications and APIs under their terms of use,
and the open-weight models were used under their licences. We name the judges
that recognise their own texts so that practitioners can take this into account.
The results apply to specific model versions and may not hold for later
versions of the same models.
