\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Language models are now routinely used to grade other language models, in
leaderboards, reward models and automated monitors. The judge and the models
it grades usually come from the same small set of systems, and judges are
known to favour their own outputs
\citep{panickssery2024selfpreference, wataoka2024perplexity, ye2025justice}.
The standard defence is to hide which model wrote each answer. That defence
only works if the judge cannot tell from the text itself that it wrote it.

This is usually checked with a single number: how often a judge names
itself as the author of its own text, compared with chance. We call this the
\emph{self-naming rate}. On its own it cannot answer the question. A judge
that names itself on 87\% of its own answers may recognise its writing. But
the text may also be easy for \emph{any} model to attribute, or the judge
may simply say ``me'' to everything. Two more numbers from the same
experiment tell these cases apart (Figure~\ref{fig:lead}):
\begin{itemize}
\item the \textbf{false-alarm rate}: how often the judge names itself on
text it did \emph{not} write;
\item the \textbf{peer baseline}: how often the \emph{other} judges name it
on its text.
\end{itemize}
We say a judge recognises its own text only if it names itself more often on
its own text than on others' (it \emph{discriminates}) and more often than
its peers do (it has a \emph{self-advantage}).

We apply this test to nine judges. Five frontier models (GPT-5.5, Claude
Opus 4.7, Gemini 3.5 Flash, Grok 4.3 and DeepSeek) attribute 190 essays they
wrote in their chat apps, and 600 texts they wrote through the API in four
genres (essays, Python code, short answers and math word problems). Four
open-weight models (Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B and Phi-4-mini)
attribute 480 texts they wrote for the same 120 prompts; with their weights we
can also read how likely each judge finds each text. The judges answer in
three formats: picking authors for a \emph{lineup} of answers to the same
prompt, naming the author of \emph{one text at a time}, and answering
\emph{``Did you write this text?''}.

\paragraph{Findings.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Most apparent self-recognition is not real.} The usual check
finds self-recognition in \SVPStd{} of \SVPCells{} combinations of judge,
text set and format; only \SVPProper{} pass our test
(Table~\ref{tab:verdicts}). Most of the others are judges that name
themselves on much of what they read: one text at a time, Llama names itself
on every text and Qwen on \OWQwenSingleNamesSelf{}. Asked ``Did you write
this text?'', Mistral says yes to \OWMistralBinaryFA{} of texts it did not
write, while GPT, Grok and DeepSeek say no to every text, their own included.
\item \textbf{Real self-recognition is rare and depends on the texts.} Only
Claude and GPT pass, and only on the chat-app essays. On the 600 API texts no
judge beats its peers, although Claude still tells its own text from others'.
\item \textbf{The information is there, but the judges do not use it.} A
simple classifier over 18 style features identifies the author of a chat-app
essay \EngineAAcc{} of the time, better than any judge. Each open-weight
judge finds its own text the most likely one on every prompt, yet what it
claims as its own does not follow that likelihood.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope.} We study one question: can an LLM judge, shown
anonymised text, tell which texts it wrote? We test this zero-shot, the way
anonymised judging pipelines use judges, with no examples and no access to
probabilities. We do not measure whether judges \emph{prefer} their own
outputs when scoring them (self-preference), and we do not train detectors.
All texts are in English and every prompt fixes the content of the answer,
so that the answers differ mainly in style. Our results describe particular
model versions at particular dates in 2026.
