\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

LLM judges now rank systems on leaderboards, label preference data for reward
models, and review the actions of other models. The judge and the models it
evaluates often come from the same few developers, and judges have been shown
to rate their own outputs higher than those of other models
\citep{panickssery2024selfpreference, wataoka2024perplexity, ye2025justice}.
A common safeguard is to remove the author's name before judging. This
safeguard works only if the judge cannot identify its own outputs from the
text, an ability usually called \emph{self-recognition}.

If a judge can recognise its own text, anonymisation cannot be relied on to
prevent self-preference: a leaderboard may favour the judge's model, a reward
model may learn the judge's style, and a monitor that reviews a copy of itself
may not be independent of it \citep{khullar2026selfattribution}. If judges
cannot recognise their own text, recognition is unlikely to be the main source
of the self-preference reported in prior work
\citep{bai2025selfrecognition}, and hiding authorship may not address that
source. Self-preference may also arise without recognition, for example when
a judge favours text that is likely under its own model
\citep{wataoka2024perplexity}. We measure recognition, not self-preference, so
our results concern one possible route to self-preference and say nothing
about its size.

The simplest measure of self-recognition is the rate at which a judge names
itself as the author of its own texts, compared with chance
\citep{davidson2024anonymized}. We call this the \emph{self-naming rate} and
the comparison the \emph{self-naming test}. A high self-naming rate has three
possible causes: (1)~the judge recognises its own writing, (2)~it names itself
on most texts whatever their author, or (3)~the texts carry traits of their
author that make them easy for any model to attribute. Only the first provides evidence specific to self-recognition.
Prior work addresses the second. Paired designs ask the judge to choose
between its own text and another
\citep{panickssery2024selfpreference, ackerman2025inspection}, and recent
one-text studies weight own and other texts equally or report balanced
accuracy \citep{stamand2026sgtr, barkhordar2026stylenotself}. Each of these
corrects for a judge's overall tendency to claim texts, which we measure as
the \emph{false-alarm rate}, the share of other models' texts on which the
judge names itself. None addresses the third cause, that any model could
attribute the texts. We add a \emph{peer
baseline}, the share of the judge's texts that the other judges attribute to
it, which is available from the same experiment. The peer baseline reflects
\emph{other-recognition}, how well other models identify a model's text,
together with each peer's general tendency to name that model; a
like-for-like version separates the two. The two controls answer two separate
questions. Does a judge distinguish its own texts from other models' texts?
And if it does, is that recognition specific to the judge, or do other models
identify the same texts as well?

Existing measurements of LLM self-recognition give conflicting results.
\citet{panickssery2024selfpreference} report that LLMs distinguish their own
text from that of other models and humans with accuracy above chance, and
connect this to self-preference. \citet{davidson2024anonymized} find no
consistent self-recognition in anonymised panels, and
\citet{bai2025selfrecognition} find accuracy close to chance when ten models
attribute one text at a time. \citet{barkhordar2026stylenotself} show that,
for code, attribution accuracy mainly reflects how often a model claims
authorship. These studies use different models, texts and question formats,
and none of them compares a judge's rate with how often other judges attribute
the same texts to it.

Our cross-evaluation design makes every model both an author and a judge. We
evaluate nine judges, five frontier models and four open-weight models, on
three sets of texts: 190 essays written in the chat apps of the five frontier
developers, 600 texts in four genres (essays, Python functions, short
explanations and math word problems) written through the API by the models that
serve as judges (120 per judge), and 480 texts written by the open-weight
models for the same 120 prompts. Judges attribute the texts in three formats:
a \emph{lineup} of all answers to one prompt, \emph{one text at a time}, and a
direct question, ``Did you write this text?''. This design yields more than
19,000 authorship judgments.

In our data, the answer to the first question (does a judge distinguish its
own texts from other models' texts?) is often yes, and the answer to the
second (is that recognition specific to the judge?) is never clearly yes
(Figure~\ref{fig:funnel}). The self-naming
test flags \CVflagged{} of the \CVcells{} cases. In \CVfailFA{} of them the
judge names itself about as often on texts it did not write as on its own;
\CVopenFlagged{} of these are open-weight cases, mostly one text at a time or
yes/no, four of them naming themselves on more than 85\% of all texts (Llama
on every one). In
the other \CVdisc{}, all frontier, the judge distinguishes its own texts from
others' (Claude in the yes/no format only with correction within its panel), so
removing the author's name does not fully hide authorship from it. The other
judges, however, often identify the same texts. Only \CVbothAttr{} cases, all on
the 38 chat-app essays per judge, beat the pooled peers (\CXLikeForLike{}
under the like-for-like comparison, one of them on API texts), and none is
significantly better than its strongest single peer. With 38 essays per judge, that last test can detect
only large advantages, so we read it as showing that no self-specific
advantage is established, not that none exists. Frontier judges can recognise
their own text, but none measurably better than its strongest peer. In other
words, for no judge can we show that having written a text makes it better at
recognising the text than the best other judge.

Claude and GPT show the two halves of this result. In the lineup, Claude names
itself on 33 of its 38 chat-app essays and on 14 of the 152 it did not write,
and it beats the pooled peers by \ClaudeAdvLineup{} points
\ClaudeAdvLineupCI{}. GPT, however, names Claude on 32 of the same 38 essays,
and on only 7 of the other 152, so against GPT alone Claude's advantage is
\CRClaudeVsBestPeer{} points \CRClaudeVsBestPeerCI{}: for Claude, another
judge does nearly as well. GPT beats the pooled peers by \GPTAdvLineup{}
points \GPTAdvLineupCI{} and its strongest peer, Grok, by \CXGPTvsGrok{} points
\CXGPTvsGrokCI{}, which is not significant after correction
($p=$~\CXGPTvsGrokHolm{}); GPT's chat-app essays were written by an app variant
that we cannot verify to be the judge model (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). For the
frontier judges it is mostly the peer controls that change verdicts: they
remove 4 of the 5 frontier cases that fail, and in the lineup the frontier
judges use each of the five names once in \CROneToOneChat{} of lineups, so the
discrimination test there gives the same verdict as the self-naming test.

The chat-app results do not carry over cleanly to the API texts. On the 600
API texts no case passes both controls with the pooled baseline (only Claude,
one text at a time, passes like-for-like), although Claude and Grok still
distinguish their own texts in the lineup. On the \CSprompts{} essay prompts
that the two sets share, GPT's discrimination declines. Claude's self-naming
and false-alarm rates barely change, but its peers name it more often, so the
comparison is inconclusive for Claude. In the chat-app lineup, every frontier
judge names Claude more often on Claude's essays than on other models'
essays: Claude's writing is recognisable to other models, not only to Claude.

Question format determines whether a judge names itself at all. When the
chat-app essays are shown one at a time, \SingleConcentration{} of all guesses
name GPT or Claude. When asked directly in a new conversation, GPT, Grok and
DeepSeek answer no for all 190 essays, including their own.
\citet{bai2025selfrecognition} report a similar reluctance in earlier models
from the same three developers. Most of the reasoning summaries returned for
GPT and Grok say that the judge has no memory of writing the text or did not
write it in the current conversation, so we read these answers as a stance on
the question rather than as evidence about recognition. The open-weight judges
show the opposite pattern, which \citet{bai2025selfrecognition} call
\emph{over-attribution}: Llama names itself on every text it is shown one at a
time.

Of five candidate explanations (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}), the clearest
result is negative: the texts carry more authorship information than the
judges' answers reflect. A classifier over 18 style features correctly
attributes \EngineAAcc{} of the chat-app essays. Each open-weight judge's own
likelihood ranks its text first among the four candidates on
\OWLikUncRawMin{} to \OWLikUncRawMax{} of prompts even without the prompt
(chance 25\%), yet the same judges attribute \OWLineupAcc{} of texts correctly
in the lineup. We find no evidence that judges claim the texts that are more
likely under their own model, more similar to their own answer, or rated
higher in quality, although these tests cannot exclude moderate effects. A bag-of-words classifier, which ignores word order,
attributes \BowShufAcc{} of the chat-app essays, whereas the four judges that
answered a lineup of word-shuffled essays are at chance; this condition is
incomplete (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}), so we do not read it as evidence
about which cues the judges use. The judges' written reasons invoke the same
model-specific traits for correct and incorrect self-attributions.

The paper makes three contributions.
\begin{itemize}
\item An audit that separates self-recognition from other-recognition. It
combines the false-alarm correction of recent work with a peer baseline,
which to our knowledge no earlier study of self-recognition has used, and
reports the peer control three ways: pooled, like-for-like and against the
strongest single peer. It needs only the judgments a lineup experiment
already collects. We release all texts, judgments and code; every number in
the paper can be recomputed from the released files without an API key, and the frontier experiments
cost a nominal \$53 in API fees.
\item The audit applied to \CVcells{} cases: nine judges, three sets of texts
and three question formats. It narrows the \CVflagged{} cases that the
self-naming test flags to \CVdisc{} that distinguish their own texts,
\CVbothAttr{} that beat the pooled peers (\CXLikeForLike{} like-for-like), and
none that is significantly better than its strongest single peer.
\item Evidence that the judges' answers reflect little of the authorship
information in the texts. A style classifier and the open-weight judges' own
likelihoods both carry signals that the judges' answers miss, and
the judges' stated reasons are the same for correct and incorrect
self-attributions, which suggests that the judges attribute text from a few
traits they associate with each model rather than from the full signal in it.
\end{itemize}
