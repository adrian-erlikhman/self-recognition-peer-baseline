\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

LLM judges are now a common tool for evaluating language models. They are
used to rank systems on leaderboards, to label preference data for reward
models, and to review the actions of other models. The judge and the models it
evaluates often come from the same few developers, and judges have been shown
to rate their own outputs higher than those of other models
\citep{panickssery2024selfpreference, wataoka2024perplexity, ye2025justice}.
The usual safeguard is to remove the author's name before judging. This
safeguard works only if the judge cannot identify its own outputs from the
text, an ability usually called \emph{self-recognition}.

If a judge can recognise its own text, anonymisation does not protect against
self-preference: a leaderboard may favour the judge's model, a reward model may
learn the judge's style, and a monitor that reviews a copy of itself is not
independent of it \citep{khullar2026selfattribution}. If judges cannot
recognise their own text, self-recognition cannot by itself explain the
self-preference reported in prior work, and hiding authorship may not address
its source.

Self-recognition is usually measured as the rate at which a judge names itself
as the author of its own texts, compared with chance. We call this the
\emph{self-naming rate}. A high self-naming rate has several possible causes.
The judge may recognise its own writing, the texts may be easy for any model to
attribute, or the judge may name itself on most texts regardless of who wrote
them. Only the first possibility provides evidence specific to
self-recognition. Two further rates, both available
from the same experiment, separate these cases (Figure~\ref{fig:lead}): the
\emph{false-alarm rate}, the share of other models' texts on which the judge
names itself, and the \emph{peer baseline}, the share of the judge's texts that
the other judges attribute to it.

Existing measurements of LLM self-recognition give conflicting results.
\citet{panickssery2024selfpreference} report that they do and connect this to
self-preference. \citet{davidson2024anonymized} find no consistent
self-recognition in anonymised panels, and \citet{bai2025selfrecognition} find
accuracy close to chance when ten models attribute one text at a time.
\citet{barkhordar2026stylenotself} show that, for code, attribution accuracy
mainly reflects how often a model claims authorship. These studies use
different models, texts and question formats, and none of them compares a
judge's rate with how often other judges attribute the same texts to it.

Our cross-evaluation design makes every model both an author and a judge. We
evaluate nine judges, five frontier models and four
open-weight models, on three sets of texts: 190 essays written by the frontier
models in their chat apps, 600 texts written by the same models through the API
in four genres, and 480 texts written by the open-weight models for the same
120 prompts. Judges attribute the texts in three formats: a \emph{lineup} of all
answers to one prompt, \emph{one text at a time}, and a direct question, ``Did
you write this text?''. This design yields more than 19,000 authorship
judgments.

The standard test detects self-recognition in \SVPStd{} of \SVPCells{}
combinations of judge, text set and format. Only \SVPProper{} of these pass our
test, which requires the judge to exceed its peer baseline and to name itself
more often on its own texts than on others'. Where the effect is present it is
large. In the lineup, Claude's self-advantage is \ClaudeAdvLineup{} points
\ClaudeAdvLineupCI{} and GPT's is \GPTAdvLineup{} points \GPTAdvLineupCI{}. On
the 600 API texts, no judge passes our test. The apparent evidence for
self-recognition also changes sharply with question format. When texts are
shown one at a time, \SingleConcentration{} of all guesses name GPT or Claude.
When asked directly, GPT, Grok and DeepSeek answer no for all 190 essays,
including their own. The open-weight judges show the opposite pattern, which we
call \emph{over-attribution}: Llama names itself on every text it is shown one
at a time.

We next test five candidate explanations for these attributions. The texts
contain substantial author-specific information: a classifier over 18 style
features correctly attributes \EngineAAcc{} of the chat-app essays, and on
every prompt, each open-weight judge assigns the highest relative likelihood to
its own text among the four candidates. Judges do not claim the texts that are
more likely under their own model, more similar to their own answer, or rated
higher in quality. When the words of each essay are shuffled, a bag-of-words
classifier still attributes \BowShufAcc{} of the essays, while the judges'
accuracy drops to chance. Their written reasons invoke the same model-specific
traits for correct and incorrect self-attributions.

In summary, we show that the standard self-recognition test flags many cases
that do not survive controls for false alarms and peer attribution, and we
propose a test based on these two controls that uses only data a lineup
experiment already collects. We report self-recognition for closed and open
models across four genres and three question formats, and we test five
explanations for the judges' behaviour, from which we derive recommendations
for auditing anonymised evaluation pipelines.
