\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Language models now grade other language models. They rank systems on public
leaderboards, supply the preference labels that train reward models, and
monitor other models' actions for safety. The judge and the models it grades
usually come from the same handful of systems, and judges are known to favour
their own outputs
\citep{panickssery2024selfpreference, wataoka2024perplexity, ye2025justice}.
The standard defence is anonymisation: hide which model wrote each answer.
That defence holds only if a judge cannot tell from the text itself which
answers are its own. If it can, a leaderboard can drift towards the judge's
own model, a reward model can learn the judge's style rather than quality,
and a monitor checking a copy of itself is no longer independent
\citep{khullar2026selfattribution}.

Whether anonymisation holds is usually checked with a single number: how
often a judge names itself as the author of its own text, compared with
chance. We call this the \emph{self-naming rate}. It cannot answer the
question on its own. A judge that names itself on 87\% of its own answers may
recognise its writing. But the text may also be easy for \emph{any} model to
attribute, or the judge may simply say ``me'' to everything. The first case
is a real leak; the other two are not, and a single rate cannot tell them
apart. Two more numbers from the same experiment can (Figure~\ref{fig:lead}):
\begin{itemize}
\item the \textbf{false-alarm rate}: how often the judge names itself on
text it did \emph{not} write;
\item the \textbf{peer baseline}: how often the \emph{other} judges name it
on its text.
\end{itemize}
We say a judge recognises its own text only if it names itself more often on
its own text than on others' (it \emph{discriminates}) and more often than
its peers do (it has a \emph{self-advantage}).

We run this test on nine judges, three sets of texts, three question formats, and more than
19,000 authorship judgments (Table~\ref{tab:data}). We wrote 120 prompts in
four genres: essays, Python code, short answers and math word problems. Five
frontier models (GPT-5.5, Claude Opus 4.7, Gemini 3.5 Flash, Grok 4.3 and
DeepSeek) answered all 120 through the API, and had earlier answered 38 of the
essay prompts in their consumer chat apps. Four open-weight models
(Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B and Phi-4-mini) answered all 120 as
well, which also lets us read how likely each judge finds each text. In every
set, the models that wrote the texts are the judges. They answer in three
formats: picking authors for a \emph{lineup} of answers to the same prompt,
naming the author of \emph{one text at a time}, and answering \emph{``Did you
write this text?''}.

\paragraph{Main findings}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The usual check is wrong most of the time it raises an
alarm.} It reports self-recognition in \SVPStd{} of \SVPCells{}
combinations of judge, text set and format. Only \SVPProper{} survive our
test (Table~\ref{tab:verdicts}); the other \SVPFalse{} are artefacts. Most
are judges that put their own name on much of what they read: one text at a
time, Llama names itself on every text and Qwen on \OWQwenSingleNamesSelf{};
asked ``Did you write this text?'', Mistral says yes to \OWMistralBinaryFA{}
of texts it did not write. The errors run one way: in no case did our test
find self-recognition that the usual check missed.
\item \textbf{Real self-recognition exists, and it is large, but it does not
transfer.} On the chat-app essays, Claude beats its peers by 33
points and GPT by 28: a genuine leak that
anonymisation does not stop. On 600 API texts from the same models, no judge
beats its peers. A judge certified safe, or flagged, on one set of texts says
little about the next.
\item \textbf{The question decides the answer.} The same judges on the same
essays look completely different across formats. One text at a time, they
send \SingleConcentration{} of all guesses to GPT or Claude, and three judges
never name themselves at all. Asked directly, GPT, Grok and DeepSeek deny
writing any of the 190 essays, their own included, while GPT names itself on
95\% of its own essays when given five names to choose from.
\item \textbf{The signal is there; the judges do not use it.} A simple
classifier over 18 style features identifies the author of \EngineAAcc{} of
the essays, more than any judge. Each open-weight judge finds its own text the
most likely one on every prompt, yet what it claims as its own does not follow
that likelihood. With each essay's words shuffled, a bag-of-words classifier
still attributes \BowShufAcc{} while the judges fall to chance. Today's
judges leave this information unused; a future judge that learns to use it
would recognise itself far better, so a judge that passes an audit today may
not pass it after an update.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Contributions} In summary, we make four contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item We show why the standard self-recognition check fails, and give a
corrected test, built from a peer baseline and a false-alarm rate, that needs
no data beyond what a lineup already collects (Section~\ref{sec:measure}).
\item We measure self-recognition across closed and open models, four
genres and three question formats, with more than 19,000 judgments
(Sections~\ref{sec:frontier} and~\ref{sec:openweight}).
\item We test four explanations of what judges rely on: their stated
reasons, similarity to their own writing, their own likelihoods, and quality,
and we assess the role of watermarks (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}).
\item We give a practical audit for LLM-judge pipelines
(Section~\ref{sec:recs}) and release all data and code.\footnote{\CodeURL}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Scope} We study one question: can an LLM judge, shown
anonymised text, tell which texts it wrote? We test this zero-shot, the way
anonymised judging pipelines use judges, with no examples and no access to
probabilities. We do not measure whether judges \emph{prefer} their own
outputs when scoring them (self-preference), and we do not train detectors.
All texts are in English and every prompt fixes the content of the answer,
so that the answers differ mainly in style. Our results describe particular
model versions at particular dates in 2026.
