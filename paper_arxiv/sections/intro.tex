\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

LLM judges are now a common tool for evaluating language models. They are
used to rank systems on leaderboards, to label preference data for reward
models, and to review the actions of other models. The judge and the models it
evaluates often come from the same few developers, and judges have been shown
to rate their own outputs higher than those of other models
\citep{panickssery2024selfpreference, wataoka2024perplexity, ye2025justice}.
The usual safeguard is to remove the author's name before judging. This
safeguard works only if the judge cannot identify its own outputs from the
text, an ability usually called \emph{self-recognition}.

Whether judges have this ability affects how evaluation pipelines should be
designed. If a judge can recognise its own text, anonymisation does not protect
against self-preference: a leaderboard may favour the judge's model, a reward
model may learn the judge's style, and a monitor that reviews a copy of itself
is not independent of it \citep{khullar2026selfattribution}. If judges cannot
recognise their own text, the self-preference reported in prior work has some
other cause, and hiding authorship does not address it.

Self-recognition is usually measured as the rate at which a judge names itself
as the author of its own texts, compared with chance. We call this the
\emph{self-naming rate}. A high self-naming rate has several possible causes.
The judge may recognise its own writing, the texts may be easy for any model to
attribute, or the judge may name itself on most texts regardless of who wrote
them. Only the first is self-recognition. Two further rates, both available
from the same experiment, separate these cases (Figure~\ref{fig:lead}): the
\emph{false-alarm rate}, the share of other models' texts on which the judge
names itself, and the \emph{peer baseline}, the share of the judge's texts that
the other judges attribute to it.

Prior studies disagree on whether LLMs recognise their own text.
\citet{panickssery2024selfpreference} report that they do and connect this to
self-preference. \citet{davidson2024anonymized} find no consistent
self-recognition in anonymised panels, and \citet{bai2025selfrecognition} find
accuracy close to chance when ten models attribute one text at a time.
\citet{barkhordar2026stylenotself} show that, for code, attribution accuracy
mainly reflects how often a model claims authorship. These studies use
different models, texts and question formats, and none of them compares a
judge's rate with how often other judges attribute the same texts to it.

We address this with a cross-evaluation design in which every model is both an
author and a judge. We evaluate nine judges, five frontier models and four
open-weight models, on three sets of texts: 190 essays written by the frontier
models in their chat apps, 600 texts written by the same models through the API
in four genres, and 480 texts written by the open-weight models for the same
120 prompts. Judges attribute the texts in three formats: a \emph{lineup} of all
answers to one prompt, \emph{one text at a time}, and a direct question, ``Did
you write this text?''. The study contains more than 19,000 authorship
judgments.

The standard test detects self-recognition in \SVPStd{} of \SVPCells{}
combinations of judge, text set and format. Only \SVPProper{} of these pass our
test, which requires the judge to exceed its peer baseline and to name itself
more often on its own texts than on others'. In the cases that pass, the effect
is large. In the lineup, Claude's self-naming rate exceeds its peer baseline by
\ClaudeAdvLineup{} points \ClaudeAdvLineupCI{} and GPT's by \GPTAdvLineup{}
points \GPTAdvLineupCI{}. On the 600 API texts, no judge exceeds its peer
baseline significantly. The question format also changes the results. When
texts are shown one at a time, \SingleConcentration{} of all guesses name GPT or
Claude. When asked directly, GPT, Grok and DeepSeek answer no for all 190
essays, including their own, which we call \emph{self-denial}. The open-weight
judges show the opposite pattern, \emph{over-attribution}: Llama names itself
on every text it is shown one at a time.

We then test what the judges' answers depend on. The texts contain enough
information to identify their authors: a classifier over 18 style features
attributes \EngineAAcc{} of the chat-app essays, and every open-weight judge
assigns the highest relative likelihood to its own text on every prompt.
Judges do not claim the texts that are more likely under their own model, more
similar to their own answer, or rated higher in quality. When the
words of each essay are shuffled, a bag-of-words classifier still attributes
\BowShufAcc{} of the essays, while the judges' accuracy drops to chance. Their
written reasons describe each model by a fixed set of traits, which they apply
in the same way to their own texts and to others'.

This paper makes three contributions. We show that the standard
self-recognition test produces mostly false positives in our data, and we
propose a test, based on a peer baseline and a false-alarm rate, that uses only
data a lineup experiment already collects. We report self-recognition for
closed and open models across four genres and three question formats. Finally,
we test five explanations for the judges' behaviour and give recommendations
for auditing anonymised evaluation pipelines.
