\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

LLM judges are now a common tool for evaluating language models. They are
used to rank systems on leaderboards, to label preference data for reward
models, and to review the actions of other models. The judge and the models it
evaluates often come from the same few developers, and judges have been shown
to rate their own outputs higher than those of other models
\citep{panickssery2024selfpreference, wataoka2024perplexity, ye2025justice}.
A common safeguard is to remove the author's name before judging. This
safeguard works only if the judge cannot identify its own outputs from the
text, an ability usually called \emph{self-recognition}.

If a judge can recognise its own text, anonymisation cannot be relied on to
prevent self-preference: a leaderboard may favour the judge's model, a reward
model may learn the judge's style, and a monitor that reviews a copy of itself
may not be independent of it \citep{khullar2026selfattribution}. If judges
cannot recognise their own text, recognition is unlikely to be the main source
of the self-preference reported in prior work
\citep{bai2025selfrecognition}, and hiding authorship may not address that
source. Self-preference may also arise without recognition, for example when
a judge favours text that is likely under its own model
\citep{wataoka2024perplexity}. We measure recognition, not self-preference, so
our results concern one possible route to self-preference and say nothing
about its size.

The simplest measure of self-recognition is the rate at which a judge names
itself as the author of its own texts, compared with chance
\citep{davidson2024anonymized}. We call this the \emph{self-naming rate} and
the comparison the \emph{self-naming test}. A high self-naming rate has several
possible causes. The judge may recognise its own writing, the judge may name
itself on most texts regardless of who wrote them, or the texts may be easy for
any model to attribute. Only the first provides evidence specific to
self-recognition. Prior work addresses the second. Paired designs ask the judge
to choose between its own text and another
\citep{panickssery2024selfpreference, ackerman2025inspection}, and recent
one-text studies weight own and other texts equally or report balanced accuracy
\citep{stamand2026sgtr, barkhordar2026stylenotself}. Each of these corrects for
a judge's overall tendency to claim texts, which we measure as the
\emph{false-alarm rate}, the share of other models' texts on which the judge
names itself. None addresses the third cause. We add a \emph{peer baseline},
the share of the judge's texts that the other judges attribute to it, which is
available from the same experiment (Figure~\ref{fig:funnel}).

Existing measurements of LLM self-recognition give conflicting results.
\citet{panickssery2024selfpreference} report that LLMs distinguish their own
text from that of other models and humans with accuracy above chance, and
connect this to self-preference. \citet{davidson2024anonymized} find no
consistent self-recognition in anonymised panels, and
\citet{bai2025selfrecognition} find accuracy close to chance when ten models
attribute one text at a time. \citet{barkhordar2026stylenotself} show that,
for code, attribution accuracy mainly reflects how often a model claims
authorship. These studies use different models, texts and question formats,
and none of them compares a judge's rate with how often other judges attribute
the same texts to it.

Our cross-evaluation design makes every model both an author and a judge. We
evaluate nine judges, five frontier models and four open-weight models, on
three sets of texts: 190 essays written in the chat apps of the five frontier
developers, 600 texts in four genres written through the API by the models that
serve as judges, and 480 texts written by the open-weight models for the same
120 prompts. Judges attribute the texts in three formats: a \emph{lineup} of
all answers to one prompt, \emph{one text at a time}, and a direct question,
``Did you write this text?''. This design yields more than 19,000 authorship
judgments.

The self-naming test flags \CVflagged{} of the \CVcells{} cases, and the two
controls remove \CVfail{} of them (Figure~\ref{fig:funnel}a). In \CVfailFA{}
the judge names itself about as often on texts it did not write as on its
own; \CVopenFlagged{} of these are open-weight judges, whose lineup answers
largely follow the position of a text. The other \CVdisc{} discriminate. Of
these, \CVbothAttr{} also exceed their peer baseline, \CVfailPeer{} do not,
and one, Claude in the yes/no format, has no peer baseline and is significant
only when corrected within its panel ($p=$~\FBClaudeHolm{}, against
$p=\CXClaudeYesNoHolmAll$ when corrected across all \CVcells{} cases). Among the frontier
judges the peer baseline is the control that changes verdicts. In the lineup
they use each of the five names once in \CROneToOneChat{} of lineups, so a
judge that names itself on its own text rarely names itself on others', and
the discrimination test gives the same verdict as the self-naming test. The
peer baseline removes 4 of the 5 frontier cases that fail. The cases that pass
are Claude and GPT on the chat-app essays. In the lineup, Claude names itself
on 33 of its 38 essays and on 14 of the 152 it did not write, and its
self-advantage is \ClaudeAdvLineup{} points \ClaudeAdvLineupCI{}; GPT's is
\GPTAdvLineup{} points \GPTAdvLineupCI{}. The raw peer baseline pools peers
of unequal skill and includes each peer's general tendency to name a model, so
we also report a like-for-like version, which sets each judge's
discrimination against its peers' discrimination for the same author. It
passes \CXLikeForLike{} cases: the same three, and Claude one text at a time
on both the chat-app essays and the API texts (Table~\ref{tab:verdicts}). The
choice matters most for Claude in the lineup. GPT names Claude on 32 of
Claude's 38 essays, so against GPT alone Claude's advantage is
\CRClaudeVsBestPeer{} points \CRClaudeVsBestPeerCI{}.

The chat-app results do not carry over cleanly to the API texts. On the 600
API texts no judge exceeds its peer baseline in the lineup, although Claude and
Grok still discriminate. On the \CSprompts{} essay prompts that the two sets
share, GPT's discrimination declines. Claude's self-naming and false-alarm
rates barely change, but its peers name it more often, so the comparison is
inconclusive for Claude. Claude discriminates in every setting we tested, most
weakly in the yes/no format, where the result holds only with correction
within the panel, and in the lineup and one-text formats the other
judges name Claude on \FXClaudeSinglePeer{} to \ClaudePeerSingle{} of its
texts: Claude's text is identifiable to its peers as well as to Claude.

The evidence also changes sharply with question format. When texts are shown
one at a time, \SingleConcentration{} of all guesses name GPT or Claude. When
asked directly in a new conversation, GPT, Grok and DeepSeek answer no for all
190 essays, including their own. \citet{bai2025selfrecognition} report a
similar reluctance in earlier models from the same three developers. Most of
the reasoning summaries returned for GPT and Grok say that the judge has no
memory of writing the text or did not write it in the current conversation,
so we read these answers as a stance on the question rather than as evidence
about recognition. The open-weight judges show the opposite pattern, which
\citet{bai2025selfrecognition} call \emph{over-attribution}: Llama names itself
on every text it is shown one at a time.

We next examine five candidate explanations for these attributions. The texts
carry authorship information that the judges do not use. A classifier over 18
style features correctly attributes \EngineAAcc{} of the chat-app essays. Each
open-weight judge's own likelihood ranks its text first among the four
candidates on \OWLikUncRawMin{} to \OWLikUncRawMax{} of prompts even without
the prompt (chance 25\%), yet the same judges attribute \OWLineupAcc{} of texts
correctly in the lineup. We find no evidence that judges claim the texts that
are more likely under their own model, more similar to their own answer, or
rated higher in quality. A bag-of-words classifier, which ignores word order,
attributes \BowShufAcc{} of the chat-app essays, whereas the four judges that
answered a lineup of word-shuffled essays are at chance. This condition is
incomplete: Claude's provider refused every shuffled lineup, and the reasoning
traces of Gemini and Grok show that they noticed the scrambling, so we do not
read it as evidence about which cues the judges use. The judges' written
reasons invoke the same model-specific traits for correct and incorrect
self-attributions.

The paper makes three contributions.
\begin{itemize}
\item A peer baseline for self-recognition, which to our knowledge no earlier
study of self-recognition has used. With the false-alarm correction of recent
work it gives an audit that needs only the judgments a lineup experiment
already collects. We release all texts, judgments and code; every number in
the paper can be recomputed without an API key, and the frontier experiments
cost about \$53 in API fees.
\item The audit applied to \CVcells{} cases: nine judges, three sets of texts
and three question formats. Of the \CVflagged{} cases that the self-naming
test flags, \CVbothAttr{} pass both controls (\CXLikeForLike{} under the
like-for-like peer comparison), all of them Claude or GPT, and one more,
Claude in the yes/no format, discriminates in a format without a peer
baseline, significant only within its panel.
\item Evidence that the judges' answers do not follow the authorship
information in the texts. A style classifier and the open-weight judges' own
likelihoods both carry signals that the judges leave unused, and the judges'
stated reasons are the same for correct and incorrect self-attributions.
\end{itemize}
