LLM judges are used to rank models, to provide reward signals for training,
and to monitor other models. Their outputs are usually anonymised, on the
assumption that a judge cannot recognise its own text. This assumption is
typically tested by checking whether a judge names itself as the author of its
own text more often than chance. We show that this test is unreliable: a high
self-naming rate can also arise when a text is easy for any model to attribute,
or when a judge names itself on most texts it sees. We therefore compare each
judge's self-naming rate with two quantities from the same experiment, its
false-alarm rate on texts it did not write and a \emph{peer baseline}, the rate
at which the other judges name it as the author of its texts. We evaluate nine
judges (five frontier and four open-weight models) on 1,270 texts in four
genres, collecting more than 19,000 authorship judgments. The standard test
detects self-recognition in \SVPStd{} cases, of which \SVPProper{} also
pass our test. In those cases the effect is large (Claude and GPT exceed their
peer baselines by 33 and 28 percentage points on essays written in their chat
apps), but it is absent on a second set of 600 texts from the same models, and
it depends on the question format. When asked directly whether they wrote a
text, GPT, Grok and DeepSeek answered no for all 190 essays, including their
own. A style classifier attributes 86\% of the essays, and each open-weight
judge assigns the highest relative likelihood to its own text on every prompt,
but the judges' answers do not track either signal. We discuss what
these results imply for anonymised evaluation and describe an audit that
requires no additional data.\footnote{Code and data:
