LLM judges rank models on leaderboards, supply the rewards that train them,
and monitor other models for unsafe behaviour. The standard protection
against a judge favouring its own outputs is to hide who wrote each answer,
and whether that protection holds is checked with one number: how often a
judge names itself as the author of its own text, compared with chance. We
show that this check is badly miscalibrated. A high self-naming rate can mean
the judge recognises its own writing, but it can equally mean that the text is
easy for \emph{any} model to attribute, or that the judge says ``me'' to
almost everything. We score each judge against two references the same
experiment already provides: how often it names itself on text it did not
write, and how often the \emph{other} judges name it on its text. Across nine
judges, 1,270 texts in four genres and more than 19,000 judgments, the usual
check reports self-recognition in \SVPStd{} cases; \SVPFalse{} of them do not
survive. Genuine self-recognition does exist: on chat-app essays, Claude and
GPT beat their peers by 33 and 28 points. But it disappears on a second set of
600 texts, and the question format alone can create or erase it. Asked
directly, three frontier judges deny writing any of 190 texts, their own
included. The authorship signal itself is plainly there (a simple style
classifier attributes 86\% of the essays, and every open-weight judge's
likelihoods pick out its own text on every prompt), yet the judges' answers
do not follow it. Anonymisation therefore cannot be certified once and for
all: it has to be tested per judge, on the texts the pipeline actually
judges, against a peer baseline. We release all data and an audit that needs
no extra data.
