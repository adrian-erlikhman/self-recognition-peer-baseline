LLM judges are used to rank models, to provide reward signals for training,
and to monitor other models. Their outputs are usually anonymised, on the
assumption that a judge cannot recognise its own text. The simplest test of
this assumption asks whether a judge names itself as the author of its own
texts more often than chance. A high self-naming rate can also arise when a
judge names itself on most texts it sees, which recent work corrects by
comparing own and other texts, or when a text is easy for any model to
attribute. For the second case we add a \emph{peer baseline}, the rate at which
the other judges name the judge as the author of its texts. Nine judges (five
frontier, four open-weight) attribute 1,270 texts in four genres, giving more
than 19,000 judgments. The self-naming test flags \CVflagged{} of \CVcells{}
cases. In \CVfailFA{} of them the judge names itself about as often on other
models' texts as on its own. Of the \CVdisc{} that discriminate, \CVbothAttr{}
also exceed their peer baseline, \CVfailPeer{} do not, and one (Claude, yes/no)
has no peer baseline. In the lineup, Claude and GPT exceed their peer
baselines by 33 and 28 points on essays written in their chat apps. On API
texts for the same prompts, GPT's discrimination declines and the result for
Claude is inconclusive: Claude discriminates in every setting, but its peers
also identify its texts. Asked in a new conversation whether they wrote a
text, GPT, Grok and DeepSeek answered no for all 190 essays, including their
own. A style classifier attributes 86\% of the essays, and each open-weight
judge's own token probabilities rank its text first on
\OWLikCondRawMin{}--\OWLikCondRawMax{} of prompts when the prompt is supplied
(\OWLikUncRawMin{}--\OWLikUncRawMax{} without it), but the judges' answers
align poorly with both signals. These results motivate a simple audit for
anonymised evaluation that uses only the judgments a lineup experiment already
collects.\footnote{Code and data: \CodeURL}
