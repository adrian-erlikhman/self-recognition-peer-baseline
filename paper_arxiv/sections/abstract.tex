LLM judges are increasingly used to rank models, to supply the rewards that
train them, and to monitor other models, and they are anonymised on the
assumption that they cannot recognise their own outputs. Whether they can is
usually tested with a single number: how often a judge names itself as the
author of its own text, compared with chance. We show that this test is
unreliable, because a high self-naming rate can equally reflect text that any
model can attribute or a judge that claims almost everything. We introduce a
\emph{peer baseline}, how often the other judges name a judge on its text, and
pair it with the judge's \emph{false-alarm rate} on text it did not write.
Across nine judges, 1,270 texts in four genres and more than 19,000
judgments, the usual test finds self-recognition in \SVPStd{} cases, and only
\SVPProper{} survive. Those that survive are large, with Claude and GPT
beating their peers by 33 and 28 points on chat-app essays, but they vanish on
a second set of 600 texts, and the question format alone can create or erase
them. Asked directly, three frontier judges deny writing any of 190 texts,
their own included, while open-weight judges answer by habit. The signal is
not missing: a style classifier attributes 86\% of the essays, and each
open-weight judge's likelihoods single out its own text on every prompt, yet
the judges' answers do not follow it. We conclude by discussing what this
means for anonymised evaluation and how to audit it.\footnote{Our code and data
