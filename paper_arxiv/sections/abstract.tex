Anonymised LLM evaluation assumes that a judge cannot recognise its own text.
The usual test asks whether a judge names itself as the author of its own
texts more often than chance. A judge can pass it without recognising
anything: it may name itself on most texts it sees, or its texts may be easy
for any model to attribute. We separate these causes with two controls
from data a lineup experiment already collects: the judge's false-alarm rate
on other models' texts, and a \emph{peer baseline}, the rate at which the
other judges name it as the author of its texts, which reflects
other-recognition. Nine judges (five frontier, four open-weight) attribute
1,270 texts in four genres, giving over 19,000 judgments. The self-naming test
flags \CVflagged{} of \CVcells{} cases, and the false-alarm control removes
\CVfailFA{}. In the other \CVdisc{}, all frontier, the judge distinguishes its
own texts from others' (Claude asked yes/no only with correction within its
panel), so anonymisation does not fully hide authorship from it. The other
judges, however, recognise the same texts. Only \CVbothAttr{} cases, all on 38
chat-app essays per judge, beat the pooled peers (\CXLikeForLike{} under a
like-for-like comparison), and none is significantly better than its
strongest single peer. In the lineup, Claude names itself on 33 of its 38
chat-app essays; GPT names Claude on 32. Asked directly whether they wrote a text, GPT, Grok and DeepSeek
answer no for all 190 essays, including their own. The texts carry more
authorship information than the judges' answers reflect: a style classifier
attributes 86\% of the essays, and each open-weight judge's own likelihood
ranks its text first on \OWLikUncRawMin{} to \OWLikUncRawMax{} of prompts, yet
those judges are at chance in the lineup. Frontier judges can recognise their
own text, but none measurably better than its strongest peer: much of what
looks like self-recognition is other-recognition.\footnote{Code and data: \CodeURL}
