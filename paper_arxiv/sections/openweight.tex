\section{Results: Open-Weight Judges}
\label{sec:openweight}

The frontier study has three limitations: we cannot read closed models'
probabilities, vendors may watermark their text, and the chat-app essays cover
one genre. The open-weight study removes all three, at the cost of much weaker
judges, so it complements rather than replaces the frontier results.

\subsection{Lineup, One Text and Yes/No}

\paragraph{No open-weight judge recognises itself in the lineup} Qwen and
Llama beat their peers (Table~\ref{tab:openweight}), but neither
discriminates. Qwen names itself on \OWQwenLineupSelf{} of its own texts but
also on \OWQwenLineupFA{} of the texts it did not write, and Llama names itself
\emph{more} often on others' texts than on its own. Both put their own name on
much of what they read, which is enough to beat peers who rarely name them:
exactly the false positive the usual check cannot detect. Overall lineup
accuracy is \OWLineupAcc{}, at chance, even though the style classifier
attributes the same texts at \OWClfPooled{}.

\paragraph{One text at a time and asked directly, each judge answers by
habit} Shown one text at a time, Qwen names itself on \OWQwenSingleNamesSelf{}
of all texts and Llama on every text, whoever wrote them; all four judges have
a significant self-advantage and none discriminates (Appendix~\ref{app:more}).
Asked ``Did you write this text?'', Mistral says yes to \OWMistralBinaryHit{}
of its own texts and \OWMistralBinaryFA{} of everyone else's, while Phi says
yes to \OWPhiBinaryHit{} and \OWPhiBinaryFA{}. Read as self-naming rates, these
numbers would rank the judges from near-certain self-recognition to none. The
AUC, which does not depend on how readily a judge says yes, is at or below
chance for all four (0.41 to 0.53), and Mistral's is reliably \emph{below}
chance.

\subsection{Answering the Task First}
\label{sec:onpolicy}

\input{table_x_openweight}

\citet{khullar2026selfattribution} find that a monitor's judgment shifts most
when it evaluates something it produced earlier in the same conversation. Does
that position help a judge recognise its writing? When the judge answers the
prompt and is then shown either that same answer, word for word, or a peer's
answer, every judge's AUC rises above chance (Table~\ref{tab:owyesno}). This
is a low bar, since the candidate is a copy of text already in the
conversation, and Llama's AUC is still only \OWLlamaOnPolAUC{}. The harder
test shows the judge a \emph{separate} answer of its own, so that recognising
it requires recognising its style rather than a copy. Here Qwen and Phi tell
their own answer from peers' (AUC \OWQwenOnPolResAUC{} and
\OWPhiOnPolResAUC{}), while Llama and Mistral fall reliably \emph{below}
chance (\OWLlamaOnPolResAUC{} and \OWMistralOnPolResAUC{}): seeing its own
answer to the task helps two judges and misleads the other two.

\begin{takeaway}
\textbf{Takeaway.} Open-weight judges reproduce every failure of the usual
check: self-advantages without discrimination, self-naming rates driven by
habit, and yes/no answers driven by disposition. None recognises its own text
from a fresh conversation, even though a classifier identifies the author of
\OWClfPooled{} of the same texts.
\end{takeaway}
