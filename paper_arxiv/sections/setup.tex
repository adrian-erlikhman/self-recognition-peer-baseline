\section{Experiment Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

To evaluate self-recognition systematically, we use a cross-evaluation design
in which each model serves as both an author and a judge. Every judge therefore
sees texts of its own among the texts it judges, which lets us compare how it
treats its own text with how it treats others' and with how other judges treat
its text.

\paragraph{Models}
We evaluate nine models in two panels. The \emph{frontier panel} contains
GPT-5.5, Claude Opus 4.7, Gemini 3.5 Flash, Grok 4.3 and DeepSeek's chat model,
accessed through the OpenRouter API at temperature 0. The \emph{open-weight
panel} contains Qwen2.5-7B (Alibaba), Llama-3.1-8B (Meta), Mistral-7B-v0.3
(Mistral AI) and Phi-4-mini (Microsoft), run in 4-bit on a single laptop GPU
with greedy decoding (Appendix~\ref{app:openweight}). Within each panel, the
same model writes and judges, so ``self'' always means the same model on both
sides.\footnote{Six of Claude's 38 chat-app essays were written by Claude Sonnet
4.6 rather than Opus 4.7; dropping them leaves Claude's lineup self-naming rate
at 27 of 32 (84\%). The DeepSeek endpoint could not be pinned to a snapshot.}

\paragraph{Dataset}
We build three sets of texts (Table~\ref{tab:data}) from one bank of prompts,
designed so that answers to the same prompt differ mainly in style: each prompt
fixes the topic and the points the answer must cover, and essay prompts ask for
plain paragraphs with no lists, headers or bold (Appendix~\ref{app:data}). The
\emph{chat-app essays} are 190 answers that the five frontier models wrote to 38
argumentative prompts in their consumer chat apps between 15 June and 31 July
2026. The \emph{API texts} are 600 answers the same models wrote on 1 October
2026 to 120 prompts in four genres: 60 essays (including the 38 above), 20
Python functions, 20 short explanations and 20 math word problems. The
\emph{open-weight texts} are 480 answers the open-weight models wrote to the
same 120 prompts, plus a second, independent answer to every prompt. We
generated the open-weight texts ourselves from public weights, so none carries
a watermark.

\paragraph{Task setup}
Judges never receive examples and never see the prompt, except in the
answer-first condition and the quality ratings of Section~ef{sec:mechanism}. We use three main formats. In the \emph{lineup}, a judge
sees all answers to one prompt and names an author for each; it is told it may
name the same model more than once, and the order of answers is balanced across
judges. In the \emph{one-text} format, a judge sees a single answer and names
its author from the panel. In the \emph{yes/no} format, a judge is asked ``Did
you write this text?'' in a fresh conversation. We add two conditions. In the
\emph{answer-first} condition (open-weight panel), following
\citet{khullar2026selfattribution}, the judge answers the prompt itself before
the yes/no question. In the \emph{shuffled lineup} (frontier panel, chat-app
essays), every essay's words are shuffled, which keeps the vocabulary but
removes word order and structure. Appendix~\ref{app:instructions} gives every
instruction.

\paragraph{Evaluation metrics}
For each judge we compute three rates from the same judgments: the
\emph{self-naming rate}, the share of its own texts it says it wrote; the
\emph{false-alarm rate}, the share of others' texts it says it wrote; and the
\emph{peer baseline}, the share of its texts that the other judges attribute to
it. From these we derive \emph{discrimination}, the self-naming rate minus the
false-alarm rate, and \emph{self-advantage}, the self-naming rate minus the peer
baseline. The \emph{usual test} asks only whether the self-naming rate exceeds
chance. \emph{Our test} requires both discrimination and a self-advantage.
Discrimination alone means anonymisation fails for that judge; the
self-advantage shows whether the judge knows its own text better than any other
judge could. Under random guessing, all three rates equal chance (20\% for five
candidates, 25\% for four) and both differences are zero. For the yes/no format
with open-weight judges we also report the AUC of the probability of ``yes'',
which does not depend on how readily a judge says yes.

\paragraph{Statistical analysis}
Judgments of the same prompt are not independent, so every test resamples or
permutes whole prompts or texts. Intervals are 95\% prompt-bootstrap intervals;
$p$-values come from permutation tests that reassign which judge counts as
``self'' on each text (self-advantage) or which text is the judge's own
(discrimination), with Holm correction across the judges of a panel
(Appendix~\ref{app:stats}). As a reference for how much the texts reveal, we
train a random forest on 18 style features, always testing on prompts it was
not trained on (Appendix~\ref{app:classifier}).
