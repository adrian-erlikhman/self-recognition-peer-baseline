\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

We use a cross-evaluation design: each model writes texts and also judges the
texts of all models in its panel, including its own. For every judge, this
gives its self-attributions on its own texts, its self-attributions on other
models' texts, and its peers' attributions on its texts.

\paragraph{Models}
We evaluate two panels. The frontier panel consists of GPT-5.5, Claude Opus
4.7, Gemini 3.5 Flash, Grok 4.3 and DeepSeek's chat model, queried through the
OpenRouter API at temperature 0. The open-weight panel consists of Qwen2.5-7B
(Alibaba), Llama-3.1-8B (Meta), Mistral-7B-v0.3 (Mistral AI) and Phi-4-mini
(Microsoft), run with 4-bit weights on a single laptop GPU with greedy decoding
(Appendix~\ref{app:openweight}). Within a panel, the judge named ``self'' is
the model under whose name a text was written. For the API and open-weight
texts the writer and the judge are the same model by construction. For the chat-app essays this holds up to the
product label shown in each application.\footnote{The chat-app essays were
recorded as written by ``Limited GPT-5.5 Instant'', ``Fast Grok 4.3'',
``Deepseek Instant'', ``Gemini 3.5 Flash'' and ``Claude Opus 4.7'', whereas the
judges are the API models listed in Appendix~\ref{app:data}. We do not know
whether the GPT, Grok and DeepSeek application variants share weights and
settings with the corresponding API models. Six of Claude's 38 chat-app essays
were written by Claude Sonnet 4.6 rather than Opus 4.7; excluding them,
Claude's lineup self-naming rate is 27 of 32 (84\%). The DeepSeek endpoint
could not be pinned to a fixed snapshot.}

\paragraph{Texts}
Table~\ref{tab:data} summarises the three sets of texts. Each prompt specifies
the topic and the points the answer must cover, to reduce variation in content
across answers to the same prompt, and essay prompts ask for plain paragraphs
without lists, headers or bold text (examples in Appendix~\ref{app:data}). The
chat-app essays are 190 answers by the five frontier models to 38 argumentative
prompts, written in each developer's consumer chat app between 15 June and 31
July 2026. The API texts are 600 answers by the five models that serve as
judges, written through the API on 1 October 2026, to 120 prompts: 60 essay prompts (including the 38 above), 20 Python
functions, 20 short explanations and 20 math word problems. The open-weight
texts are 480 answers by the four open-weight models to the same 120 prompts,
plus a second, independently sampled answer to each prompt. We generated the
open-weight texts from public weights, so they carry no watermark.

\paragraph{Question formats}
Judges receive no examples. They see the original prompt only in the
answer-first condition and in the quality ratings of
Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}. In the \emph{lineup} format, the judge sees all
answers to one prompt and names an author for each; the instructions state that
the same model may be named more than once. The answers appear in one of five
orders, the rotations of the order in which the instructions list the
candidates (GPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok, DeepSeek), so that every judge sees each
model's answer in each position equally often; the open-weight lineups rotate
their own list of four in the same way. Judges tend to assign names in the
listed order: on the chat-app essays, lineup accuracy is \CRAccListedChat{}
when the answers follow the listed order and \CRAccOtherChat{} otherwise.
Because positions are balanced, this raises the self-naming rate, the
false-alarm rate and the peer baseline together and leaves their differences
nearly unchanged (Table~\ref{tab:anchor} in Appendix~\ref{app:more}). In the
\emph{one-text} format, the judge sees a single answer and names its author. In the \emph{yes/no} format, the judge is asked ``Did you write this
text?'' in a new conversation. In the \emph{answer-first} condition (open-weight
panel only), following \citet{khullar2026selfattribution}, the judge first
answers the prompt and is then asked the yes/no question. In the \emph{shuffled
lineup} (frontier panel, chat-app essays only), the words of each essay are
randomly reordered, which preserves the vocabulary but removes word order and
paragraph structure. Appendix~\ref{app:instructions} lists all instructions.

\paragraph{Measures}
For each judge we compute three rates from the same set of judgments. The
\emph{self-naming rate} is the share of the judge's own texts that it
attributes to itself. The \emph{false-alarm rate} is the share of other models'
texts that it attributes to itself. The \emph{peer baseline} is the share of the
judge's texts that the other judges attribute to it. \emph{Discrimination} is
the self-naming rate minus the false-alarm rate, the difference between hit and
false-alarm rates used in signal detection \citep{green1966signal}, and
\emph{self-advantage} is the self-naming rate minus the peer baseline. The \emph{self-naming test} asks whether the
self-naming rate exceeds chance in a one-sided binomial test (in the yes/no
format, whether the judge answers yes for more than half of its own texts); a
case that passes is \emph{flagged}. We apply two controls to each case. A judge
\emph{discriminates} if its discrimination is positive and statistically
significant, and it \emph{exceeds its peers} if its self-advantage is positive
and statistically significant. Discrimination indicates that the judge
distinguishes its own texts from others', which weakens the protection
anonymisation provides whether or not other judges can do the same. A
self-advantage indicates that the judge identifies its own texts more often
than the other judges do on average. The peer baseline pools judges of unequal
ability and includes each peer's general tendency to name that model, so
Appendix~\ref{app:more} reports how often each judge names Claude and GPT
(Table~\ref{tab:naming}). We reserve the term self-recognition for cases that
pass both controls. The yes/no format has no peer baseline, because each judge
is asked only about itself, so these cases can be tested for discrimination
only and we count them separately. Under random guessing all three rates equal chance (20\% with five
candidates, 25\% with four) and both differences are zero. For open-weight
judges in the yes/no format we also report the AUC of the probability of
``yes'', which does not depend on a judge's overall tendency to answer yes.

\paragraph{Statistical tests}
Judgments that share a prompt are not independent, so all tests resample or
permute at the level of prompts or texts. We report 95\% confidence intervals
from a bootstrap over prompts. Self-advantage is tested by permuting which judge
is labelled ``self'' on each text, and discrimination by permuting which text
in a lineup is the judge's own. $p$-values are Holm-corrected across the judges
in a panel (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}). As a reference for how much authorship
information the texts contain, we train a random forest on 18 style features and
always evaluate it on prompts it was not trained on
(Appendix~\ref{app:classifier}).
