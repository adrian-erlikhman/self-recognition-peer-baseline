\section{Analysis: What Do the Judges Rely On?}
\label{sec:mechanism}

The texts carry enough information to identify their authors, yet the judges
mostly fail to use it. We look at what the judges say, and then test four
explanations of what drives their answers.

\subsection{Stated Reasons}
\label{sec:rationales}

Every frontier judgment on the chat-app essays came with a one-sentence
reason. We coded all 1,900 reasons into eleven types of cue (length, tone,
structure, word choice, hedging, ``typical of model X'', and so on) using
rules checked against two LLM annotators on a sample of 150
(Appendix~\ref{app:rationales}).

\emph{No judge ever says ``this is how I write''.} Judges name themselves in
the third person, as they name any model: 46\% of lineup reasons and 95\% of
one-text reasons appeal to a model's typical style (``classic Claude
hedging''), including the 66 in which Claude correctly named itself.

\emph{The reasons are the same whether a judge is right or wrong.} When
Claude names itself on its own text one at a time, it cites hedging in 31 of
33 cases; when it names itself on text it did not write, in 12 of 13. This is
what a judge applying a stereotype of itself would produce, although stated
reasons cannot prove what drove a decision \citep{marioriyad2025silent}.

\emph{The reasons are mostly accurate about the text.} When a lineup reason
calls an answer long, it is the longest of the five in 47 of 71 cases, and
quoted phrases appear in the text in 238 of 245 cases. The reasons describe
the text correctly; they just do not separate a judge's own text from
others'.

\subsection{Similarity to Their Own Writing}
\label{sec:genmatch}

\citet{wataoka2024perplexity} suggest that a judge favours, and perhaps
claims, text it would have written itself. For the frontier judges we use a
judge's own answer to the same prompt as ``what it would have written'', and
ask whether it names itself more often on other models' texts that resemble
that answer. Across four similarity measures, 1 of 36 tests reaches
$p<0.05$, about what chance gives. Nor do judges, when wrong, name the model
whose answer is most similar to the text (31.9\% of 630 wrong lineup answers,
against 30.1\% expected), although the same similarity measures identify the
true author 74--79\% of the time.

\subsection{Their Own Likelihoods}
\label{sec:likelihood}

With open weights we can read this directly. For every judge and text we
compute how likely the text is under the judge, relative to the other three
judges. On every prompt, the text each judge finds relatively most likely is
its own (\OWLikCondAccMin{}; chance 25\%). This is expected, since each text
was sampled from its author, but it shows that the information is available
in the judge's probabilities. Yet what a judge \emph{claims} as its own does
not follow it: among the texts a judge did not write, those it claims are no
more likely under it than those it does not (none of \OWFamTests{} tests
survives correction; the strongest effect goes the wrong way). A judge does
not see its peers' likelihoods when it judges, so this information may not be
usable in practice.

\subsection{Quality}
\label{sec:quality}

\citet{stamand2026sgtr} show that a judge can appear to recognise itself by
claiming whichever text it thinks is best. We had the four open-weight models
rate every chat-app essay and every open-weight text for quality on a 1--9
scale. The frontier judges do not claim the better-rated texts, and
controlling for quality leaves Claude's, GPT's and Grok's lineup
self-advantages significant. The control is coarse: almost every frontier
essay is rated near the top of the scale, and the raters agree only weakly.
The open-weight judges also show no self-preference: relative to how its
peers rate the same texts, no judge rates its own texts higher than it rates
others' (largest difference \OWQualSelfPrefMax{} points on the 9-point scale,
every interval including zero).

\subsection{Watermarks}
\label{sec:watermark}

Could a judge be reading a watermark its vendor planted? Three observations
argue against it. First, deployed watermarks are designed
to be undetectable without a secret key, which the judges do not have.
Second, the chat-app essays were written between 15 June and 31 July 2026,
before Anthropic announced watermarking of Claude text in August
\citep{anthropic2026watermark, anthropic2026marking}, so Claude's
self-advantage comes from text written before any announced Claude watermark.
Conversely, Gemini-app text is watermarked \citep{deepmind2026synthid}, and
Gemini does not recognise itself. Third, the open-weight texts carry no
watermark, yet a classifier still attributes them at \OWClfPooled{}: the
signal is stylistic. (The shuffled-word test of Section~\ref{sec:frontier}
would also defeat a watermark, but there the judges lost all ability to
attribute anything, so it cannot separate the two.)

\begin{takeaway}
\textbf{Takeaway.} The information a judge would need to recognise itself is
already present, in the text's style and in the judge's own probabilities,
yet the judges' answers do not track it. Nor do they track similarity to the
judge's own writing or the quality of the text, and watermarks cannot explain
the results. The judges' stated reasons suggest stereotypes of each model,
applied to every text alike.
\end{takeaway}
