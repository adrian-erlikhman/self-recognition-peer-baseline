\section{Why Do Judges Fail to Recognise Themselves?}
\label{sec:mechanism}
\label{sec:watermark}

In this section, we explore why most judges fail to recognise their own text,
and why the few that succeed do so only on some texts. We consider five
possible explanations:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The texts carry too little signal.} If answers to the same prompt
were stylistically indistinguishable, no judge could recognise its own.
\item \textbf{Judges match their own way of writing.} A judge may claim text
that resembles what it would itself have written
\citep{wataoka2024perplexity}.
\item \textbf{Judges follow their own likelihood.} A judge may claim text that
is familiar, that is, likely under its own distribution.
\item \textbf{Judges claim the best text.} A judge may name itself on whichever
text it rates highest \citep{stamand2026sgtr}.
\item \textbf{Judges read a watermark.} A judge could in principle detect a
signal its vendor planted in the text.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The signal is present, but the judges do not use it}
A random forest over 18 style features attributes \EngineAAcc{} of the chat-app
essays (95\% CI \EngineACI{}) and \OWClfPooled{} of the open-weight texts, more
than any judge achieves. To see what the judges rely on, we repeated the lineup
with every essay's words shuffled. The remaining judges fall to chance
(accuracy \FSAcc{}; Figure~\ref{fig:shuffle}, Table~\ref{tab:fshuffle}), and no
self-advantage or discrimination is significant, while a bag-of-words
classifier still attributes \BowShufAcc{} of the shuffled essays, in line with
\citet{sun2025stylometric}. Claude's provider refused all 38 of its shuffled
lineups with a content filter, and Gemini gave no answer on \FSGeminiMissing{}
of its 38; we treat these as missing. The judges depend on reading the text as
prose and do not exploit the word statistics a classifier uses.

\paragraph{Similarity to their own writing does not predict self-naming}
For the frontier judges we take a judge's own answer to the same prompt as
``what it would have written'' and ask whether it names itself more often on
others' texts that resemble that answer. Across four similarity measures
(style features, word and character $n$-grams, function words), 1 of 36 tests
reaches $p<0.05$, about what chance gives. Nor do judges, when wrong, name the
model whose answer is most similar to the text (31.9\% of 630 wrong lineup
answers, against 30.1\% expected), although the same measures identify the true
author 74--79\% of the time.

\paragraph{Likelihood singles out the judge's own text, but its answers ignore
it} For each open-weight judge and text we compute how likely the text is
under the judge relative to the other three judges. On every prompt, the text
each judge finds relatively most likely is its own (\OWLikCondAccMin{}; chance
25\%). This is expected, since each text was sampled from its author, but it
shows that the information is present in the judge's own probabilities. Yet
among the texts a judge did not write, those it claims are no more likely under
it than those it does not: none of \OWFamTests{} tests survives correction, and
the strongest effect goes the wrong way (Appendix~\ref{app:more}).

\paragraph{Quality does not explain self-naming}
The four open-weight models rated every chat-app essay and open-weight text for
quality on a 1--9 scale. The frontier judges do not claim the better-rated
essays, and controlling for quality leaves Claude's, GPT's and Grok's lineup
self-advantages significant. The control is coarse, since almost every frontier
essay is rated near the top of the scale. The open-weight judges show no
self-preference: relative to their peers, none rates its own texts higher than
others' (largest difference \OWQualSelfPrefMax{} points, every interval
including zero).

\paragraph{Watermarks cannot explain the results}
Deployed watermarks are designed to be undetectable without a secret key, which
the judges do not have. The chat-app essays were written between 15 June and 31
July 2026, before Anthropic announced watermarking of Claude text in August
\citep{anthropic2026watermark, anthropic2026marking}, so Claude's self-advantage
comes from text written before any announced Claude watermark. Conversely,
Gemini-app text is watermarked \citep{deepmind2026synthid}, and Gemini does not
recognise itself. The open-weight texts carry no watermark, yet a classifier
attributes them at \OWClfPooled{}.

\paragraph{Stated reasons reveal stereotypes, not self-knowledge}
Every frontier judgment came with a one-sentence reason, and we coded all 1,900
(Appendix~\ref{app:rationales}). No judge ever refers to itself (``this is how I
write''); judges describe every model in the third person, and 95\% of one-text
reasons appeal to a model's typical style. The reasons are the same whether a
judge is right or wrong (Table~\ref{tab:reasons}): when Claude names itself one
text at a time, it cites hedging in 31 of 33 correct cases and in 12 of 13
incorrect ones. Grok and Gemini attribute their own essays to Claude, citing
``polished'' and ``elegant'' prose as Claude's typical style, which echoes the
hierarchical bias toward frontier families reported by
\citet{bai2025selfrecognition}. The reasons are mostly accurate about the
text, but they apply a stereotype of each model to every text alike, which is
why they rarely separate a judge's own writing from others'.
