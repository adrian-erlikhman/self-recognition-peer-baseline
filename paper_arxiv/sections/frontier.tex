\section{Frontier judges}
\label{sec:frontier}

\subsection{Setup}

\paragraph{Texts.} Five frontier models (GPT-5.5, Claude Opus 4.7, Gemini 3.5
Flash, Grok 4.3 and DeepSeek's chat model) each answered the same 39
argumentative prompts in their consumer chat apps at default settings,
between 15 June and 31 July 2026. Each prompt fixes the topic and the three
points the answer must make, and asks for plain paragraphs with no lists,
headers or bold, so the five answers to a prompt cover the same content and
differ mainly in style (Appendix~\ref{app:data} gives an example). One prompt
was dropped because two of its answers were corrupted copies, leaving 38
prompts and 190 essays. We call these the \emph{chat-app texts}.

\paragraph{Judges and formats.} The same five models judged through
OpenRouter at temperature~0, so ``self'' means the same model on both
sides.\footnote{Six of Claude's 38 essays were written by Claude Sonnet 4.6
rather than Opus 4.7; dropping them leaves Claude's lineup self-naming rate at
27 of 32 (84\%). The DeepSeek endpoint could not be pinned to a snapshot.}
Judges see only the answers, never the prompts. In the \emph{lineup}, a judge
sees all five answers to one prompt, labelled A to E, and names an author for
each; it is told that it may name the same model more than once, and the
order of answers is balanced across judges. In the \emph{one-text} format, a
judge sees a single answer and names its author from the same five models.
Each format gives 950 judgments. The exact instructions are in
Appendix~\ref{app:instructions}.

\paragraph{A reference point.} To check how much the texts reveal about
their authors, we trained a random forest on 18 simple style features (length,
punctuation, vocabulary richness, readability), always testing on prompts it
was not trained on. It identifies the author of \EngineAAcc{} of the essays
(95\% CI \EngineACI{}; chance \Chance{}), more than any judge does
(Appendix~\ref{app:classifier}).

\subsection{Results on the chat-app texts}

\input{table_frontier}

\paragraph{In the lineup, two judges pass and one trails its peers.}
Table~\ref{tab:frontier} shows every quantity from Section~\ref{sec:measure}.
Claude's self-advantage is \ClaudeAdvLineup{} points \ClaudeAdvLineupCI{} and
GPT's is \GPTAdvLineup{} \GPTAdvLineupCI{}. Both keep false alarms near
10\%, so both also discriminate. DeepSeek and Gemini show no advantage. Grok
does significantly \emph{worse} on its own text than its peers do
(\GrokAdvLineup{} \GrokAdvLineupCI{}): it names itself on only 2 of its 38
essays. The verdicts for Claude, GPT and Grok hold under every analysis we
tried, including mixed-effects models and an adjustment for how good each
judge is at attribution in general (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}). Gemini's
does not: it is among the best at attributing \emph{other} models' text, so
after that adjustment it too trails its peers on its own.

\paragraph{One text at a time, the format sets the answer.} Shown a single
text, the judges send \SingleConcentration{} of all 950 guesses to GPT or
Claude, as \citet{bai2025selfrecognition} also found. Gemini, Grok and
DeepSeek never name themselves at all, so for them the format measures
nothing. GPT's self-naming rate rises to \GPTSelfSingle{}, but it also names
itself on half the texts it did not write. Claude keeps its self-naming rate
(\ClaudeSelfSingle{}) but loses its self-advantage (\ClaudeAdvSingle{}),
because its peers now name Claude on \ClaudePeerSingle{} of Claude's texts.
The peer baseline is only informative when judges spread their guesses.

\subsection{Asking directly: ``Did you write this text?''}
\label{sec:frontiernew}

\input{table_g_frontier_yesno}

We also asked each judge, about each of the 190 essays and in a fresh
conversation, whether it wrote the text (Table~\ref{tab:fyesno}). GPT, Grok
and DeepSeek answered ``No'' to all 190, including their own 38. Gemini said
yes twice, both times to text it did not write. Only Claude ever claims its
own text: it says yes to \FBClaudeHitK{} of its own essays and to
\FBClaudeFAK{} of the others', so it discriminates (\FBClaudeDisc{} points,
Holm $p=$~\FBClaudeHolm{}), but it also claims half of what it did not write.
GPT, which names itself on 95\% of its own texts when it has five names to
choose from, never claims one when asked directly. The question, not the
text, sets the answer.

\subsection{Shuffling the words}

\input{table_g_frontier_shuffle}

Do the judges rely on word choice, or on word order and structure? We
repeated the lineup with every essay's words shuffled, which keeps the
vocabulary and punctuation but destroys sentences and paragraphs. Claude's
provider refused all 38 of Claude's shuffled lineups with a content filter (a
repeat on Anthropic's own endpoint was refused too), and Gemini gave no
answer on \FSGeminiMissing{} of its 38; we treat these as missing. The
remaining judges are at chance (accuracy \FSAcc{}; Table~\ref{tab:fshuffle}),
and no self-advantage or discrimination is significant. Yet the word
distribution alone still identifies the author: a simple bag-of-words
classifier attributes \BowShufAcc{} of the shuffled essays, matching
\citet{sun2025stylometric}. The judges depend on reading the text as
prose; they do not use the word statistics a classifier uses.

\subsection{A larger and more varied set of texts}
\label{sec:frontier120}

\input{table_frontier120}

Thirty-eight essays per judge is a small sample, and essays are one genre.
We therefore had the same five models answer 120 prompts through the API
(temperature 0.7, no system prompt) in four genres: 60 essays (including the
38 prompts above), 20 Python functions, 20 short explanations and 20 math
word problems. The same five judges attributed these 600 \emph{API texts}
(Table~\ref{tab:frontier120}).

\paragraph{No judge beats its peers, but Claude still discriminates.} In
the lineup, no self-advantage is significant. Claude names itself on
\FXClaudeLineupSelf{} of its own texts and only \FXClaudeLineupFA{} of
others' (discrimination \FXClaudeLineupDisc{} points), but its peers now name
Claude on \FXClaudeLineupPeer{} of its texts, almost as often. Grok, which
trailed its peers on the chat-app essays, now discriminates too
(\FXGrokLineupDisc{}). One text at a time, the judges again send
\FXSingleTopTwo{} of their guesses to GPT or Claude, and GPT names itself on
\FXGPTSingleNamesSelf{} of all texts, whoever wrote them.

\paragraph{What changed.} The API texts differ from the chat-app essays in
how they were collected, decoding settings, date and genre. On the 38 prompts
the two sets share, Claude's and GPT's self-advantages keep their sign but
shrink (\FXOldClaudeLineupAdv{} and \FXOldGPTLineupAdv{} points) and are no
longer significant; Claude's peers name its API essays more often. On the 82
new prompts no judge's self-advantage reaches 10 points, while Claude's
discrimination holds. By genre, Claude's discrimination is largest on essays
and absent on math, where answers to the same problem share most of their
words (Appendix~\ref{app:more}). The lesson is that self-advantage depends on
the texts: the same judges that beat their peers on one set of essays do not
on another.
