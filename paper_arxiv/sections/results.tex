\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
\label{sec:frontier}
\label{sec:openweight}

Our evaluation shows that most apparent self-recognition in LLM judges is an
artefact of the measurement. Three findings emerge. First, the usual test flags
self-recognition far more often than our test confirms it. Second, genuine
self-recognition exists but depends on the texts and the question format.
Third, open-weight judges reproduce every failure of the usual test.

\input{table_verdicts}

\paragraph{The usual test mostly flags self-recognition that is not there}
Table~\ref{tab:verdicts} applies both tests to every judge, set of texts and
format. The usual test finds self-recognition in \SVPStd{} of \SVPCells{}
cases; only \SVPProper{} pass our test. The other \SVPFalse{} are judges that
put their own name on much of what they read, or whose peers name them just as
often. The errors run one way: in no case did our test find self-recognition
that the usual test missed.

\paragraph{In the lineup, Claude and GPT genuinely recognise their own essays}
On the chat-app essays, Claude names itself on 33 of its 38 essays (87\%) and
on only 14 of the 152 essays it did not write (9\%), while its peers name
Claude on 54\% of Claude's essays (Table~\ref{tab:frontier},
Figure~\ref{fig:lead-lineup}). Its self-advantage is \ClaudeAdvLineup{} points
\ClaudeAdvLineupCI{}; GPT's is \GPTAdvLineup{} \GPTAdvLineupCI{}. Both
discriminate, so these are genuine leaks that anonymisation does not stop.
DeepSeek and Gemini show no advantage, and Grok does significantly \emph{worse}
on its own essays than its peers do (\GrokAdvLineup{} \GrokAdvLineupCI{}),
naming itself on only 2 of 38. The verdicts for Claude, GPT and Grok hold under
mixed-effects models and after adjusting for each judge's general skill at
attribution (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}).

\paragraph{One text at a time, predictions collapse onto GPT and Claude}
Shown a single essay, the judges send \SingleConcentration{} of their guesses
to GPT or Claude (Figure~\ref{fig:guess}), consistent with
\citet{bai2025selfrecognition}. Gemini, Grok and DeepSeek never name themselves
at all, so for them this format measures nothing. GPT's self-naming rate rises
to \GPTSelfSingle{}, but it also names itself on half of the essays it did not
write. Claude keeps its self-naming rate (\ClaudeSelfSingle{}) but loses its
self-advantage (\ClaudeAdvSingle{}), because its peers now name Claude on
\ClaudePeerSingle{} of Claude's essays (Figure~\ref{fig:lead-single}). The same
judges on the same essays give a very different answer, and the peer baseline
is informative only when judges spread their guesses.

\paragraph{Asked directly, most frontier judges deny authorship}
Asked ``Did you write this text?'' about each of the 190 essays, GPT, Grok and
DeepSeek answer ``No'' to all of them, including their own 38
(Figure~\ref{fig:yes-frontier}). Gemini says yes twice, both times to essays it
did not write. We describe this as \emph{self-denial}: these judges exclude
themselves from consideration altogether. Only Claude ever claims its own
text: it says yes to \FBClaudeHitK{} of its own essays and \FBClaudeFAK{} of
others', so it discriminates (\FBClaudeDisc{} points, Holm
$p=$~\FBClaudeHolm{}) while also claiming half of what it did not write.
Strikingly, GPT, which names itself on 95\% of its own essays when given five
names to choose from, never claims one when asked directly.

\paragraph{Self-recognition does not transfer to new texts}
On the 600 API texts, no judge's lineup self-advantage is significant
(Table~\ref{tab:frontier120}). Claude still discriminates: it names itself on
\FXClaudeLineupSelf{} of its own texts and \FXClaudeLineupFA{} of others'. But
its peers now name Claude on \FXClaudeLineupPeer{} of its texts, almost as
often. Grok, which trailed its peers on the chat-app essays, now discriminates
too (\FXGrokLineupDisc{}). Even on the 38 prompts the two sets share, Claude's
and GPT's self-advantages shrink (\FXOldClaudeLineupAdv{} and
\FXOldGPTLineupAdv{} points) and are no longer significant. Claude's
discrimination is largest on essays and absent on math, where answers to the
same problem share most of their words (Appendix~\ref{app:more}). A judge
certified safe, or flagged, on one set of texts says little about the next.

\paragraph{Open-weight judges answer by habit}
No open-weight judge recognises itself in the lineup
(Table~\ref{tab:openweight}). Qwen and Llama beat their peers but do not
discriminate: Qwen names itself on \OWQwenLineupSelf{} of its own texts and
\OWQwenLineupFA{} of others', and Llama names itself more often on others'
texts than on its own. One text at a time, Qwen names itself on
\OWQwenSingleNamesSelf{} of all texts and Llama on every text, a pattern we
call \emph{over-attribution}. Asked directly, Mistral says yes to
\OWMistralBinaryHit{} of its own texts and \OWMistralBinaryFA{} of others',
while Phi says yes to \OWPhiBinaryHit{} and \OWPhiBinaryFA{}
(Figure~\ref{fig:yes-open}). Read as self-naming rates, these numbers would rank
the judges from near-certain self-recognition to none; the AUC, which ignores
how readily a judge says yes, is at or below chance for all four (0.41 to 0.53).
Overall lineup accuracy is \OWLineupAcc{}, at chance, although the style
classifier attributes the same texts at \OWClfPooled{}.

\paragraph{Answering first helps some judges and misleads others}
\label{sec:onpolicy}
When a judge first answers the prompt and is then shown that same answer, word
for word, or a peer's, every judge's AUC rises above chance
(Table~\ref{tab:owyesno}). This is a low bar, since the candidate is a copy of
text already in the conversation. When it is shown a \emph{separate} answer of
its own instead, Qwen and Phi tell it from peers' answers (AUC
\OWQwenOnPolResAUC{} and \OWPhiOnPolResAUC{}), while Llama and Mistral fall
reliably \emph{below} chance (\OWLlamaOnPolResAUC{} and
\OWMistralOnPolResAUC{}). Seeing its own answer to the task helps two judges and
misleads the other two.
